Guide¶

Cumulant generating functions¶

A CGF wraps a Python function K(t) of a numpy vector t of length \(d\) (also for \(d = 1\), where you write t[0]), together with the dimension and, optionally, a predicate describing the domain:

K = CGF(lambda t: -3*np.log(1 - t[0]) - 2*np.log(1 - 2*t[1]), 2,
        domain=lambda t: t[0] < 1 and t[1] < 1/2)

The function is evaluated on vectors of truncated multivariate Taylor polynomials (TPoly) to generate its derivatives, so it must be written generically: arithmetic, np.exp, np.log, np.sqrt, powers, np.sum, products with constant matrices and vectors (A @ t), np.sin, np.cos, np.tan, np.sinh, np.cosh, np.tanh, np.arctan all work, and sinhc() and sincu() give \(\sinh(x)/x\) and \(\sin(x)/x\) with their removable singularity at zero (write np.log(sinhc(t[0])) for the uniform distribution, not np.log(np.sinh(t[0])/t[0]), which is \(0/0\) at the origin where the saddlepoint iteration starts); math.log, float(...) and calls into numerical libraries that require floats do not. For determinants and inverses of matrices that depend on t, write the factorization in generic arithmetic as quadratic_forms_cgf() does, or use that constructor.

Outside its domain, K may raise a DomainError (np.log and np.sqrt of a polynomial with a negative constant term do this automatically) or return a non-finite value. When the formula is finite outside the true domain, as with \(1/(1-t)\), the domain predicate is needed; it is also cheaper than an exception.

Transformations: reflect() (sign changes), marginal(), linear_combination() (\(Y = B'X\)), sample_mean() (\(nK(t/n)\)), shift() (\(X + c\)), and K1 + K2 for the sum of independent vectors. The library: normal_cgf(), gamma_cgf(), ncx2_cgf(), exponential_sums_cgf(), quadratic_forms_cgf(), wishart_diag_cgf(), common_component_cgf(), uniform_cgf().

Tail probabilities¶

tailprob() (K, x, order=M) returns the expansions of orders \(1, \dots, M\) of \(P(X \ge x)\) in F, with the saddlepoint tau, a status string and the full result of the underlying routine in info. The expansion of order \(M\) keeps the terms of orders \(n^{-m/2}\), \(m \le 2M - 1\), and has relative error \(O(n^{-M})\); in practice each order gains one to two digits at moderate sample sizes, and the first order is accurate to a few percent.

lower=True gives \(P(X \le x)\) and signs a mixed corner, e.g. signs=[1, -1] for \(P(X_1 \ge x_1, X_2 \le x_2)\). When the saddlepoint has negative components, the expansion is applied through the inclusion-exclusion identity of the paper, which preserves the relative error bound. In three dimensions the orthant integrals are computed by Gauss quadrature and in four or more by randomized quasi-Monte Carlo (info["err"] is then a standard error); keyword arguments are passed on to spa_mv() (nG, nqmc, …) and, for \(d = 2\), to spa_orthant2() (nG, logscale for probabilities below the double-precision range, …). For \(d = 1\), x may be a scalar.

Densities¶

density() (K, x, order=M) returns the expansions of orders \(1, \dots, M\) of the density (the terms \(m \le M-1\) of the expansion in powers of \(n^{-1}\), relative error \(O(n^{-M})\)); a finite sum of Hermite polynomials, no integration, any dimension. In three or more dimensions the terms can cancel strongly at high orders; check info["kappa_pdf"], the ratio of the sum of the absolute terms to the sum (about \(\log_{10}\) of it digits are lost). The Julia package repeats such computations in higher precision; this package works in double precision.

Ratios¶

For a bivariate K with a positive denominator \(X_2 > 0\): ratio_density() (K, r) is the Rice expansion of the density of \(X_1/X_2\) at \(r\) through the saddlepoint of \(K_W(s) = K(s, -rs)\), and ratio_tailprob() (K, r, lower=...) is the univariate expansion for \(W = X_1 - rX_2\) at \(0\). Example, the noncentral \(F\) with \(Q_1 \sim \chi^2_m(\lambda)\) and \(Q_2 \sim \chi^2_n\):

K = CGF(lambda t: ncx2_cgf(m, lam)(t[:1]) + ncx2_cgf(n)(t[1:]), 2,
        domain=lambda t: t[0] < 0.5 and t[1] < 0.5)
c = f*m/n
ratio_tailprob(K, c, lower=True)["F"]     # P(F <= f), orders 1..3
ratio_density(K, c)["f"] * (m/n)          # density of F at f

Several ratios. With a vector r of length \(d\), K is the cgf of \((X_1, \dots, X_d, Y_1, \dots, Y_d)\), or of \((X_1, \dots, X_d, Y)\) for a common denominator, and ratio_density returns the joint density of \((X_1/Y_1, \dots, X_d/Y_d)\) at r (Theorem 4 with the amplitude expansion of Corollary 1 of the paper, every Taylor coefficient from one polynomial of \(K(s, -r \circ s + \tau)\)), while ratio_tailprob returns the orthant probability \(P(R \ge r)\) with lower and signs as in tailprob. The sample autocorrelations at lags one and two, for example, are two ratios of quadratic forms with a common denominator:

N = 50; Mc = np.eye(N) - np.ones((N, N))/N
A = lambda j: (np.diag(np.ones(N - j), j) + np.diag(np.ones(N - j), -j))/2
K = quadratic_forms_cgf([Mc @ A(1) @ Mc, Mc @ A(2) @ Mc, Mc])
ratio_density(K, [-1, -1]/np.sqrt(N), order=5)["f"]

Closed-form Taylor polynomials¶

Automatic differentiation is the default, but a CGF can carry its Taylor polynomials in closed form through the argument taylor, a function taylor(t, N, M) returning the polynomial of degree N of \(K(t + Mv)\) as a TPoly (built from a coefficient table with from_coefficients()), or None outside the domain. quadratic_forms_cgf() uses this with the trace recursion of the paper evaluated directly in the whitened directions, which makes large matrices cheap, and the transformations carry the option along; pass closedform=False to differentiate the generic formula instead. Comparisons between polynomials, or with numbers, compare the constant terms, so a cgf written with an if on its argument can be differentiated.

Derivatives and the saddlepoint¶

derivatives() (K, t, N) returns all derivatives \(K^{(\nu)}(t)\), \(|\nu| \le N\), as a dict keyed by the multi-index, and saddlepoint() (K, x) solves \(K'(\tau) = x\) by Newton’s method with backtracking. The derivatives at the saddlepoint are generated once, to the degree \(2M + 1\) needed by order \(M\), and the whitened coefficients (of \(K_Y(u) = K(L^{-T}u)\), \(K''(\tau) = LL'\)) are generated by differentiating the composed function directly, which keeps them as accurate as the original ones.

The derivative interface¶

The engine can be used with derivatives supplied by the user, as Kfun(nu, t) returning the derivative of multi-index order nu (a tuple) at t, NaN outside the domain: spa_saddle(), spa_uni(), spa_ratio_pdf(), spa_bi(), spa_bi_at(), spa_orthant2(), spa_mv(), spa_mv_at(), spa_orthant(). These follow the Julia package and the MATLAB programs of the paper. kfun() and kwhite() turn a CGF into the functions they take.

The Taylor algebra¶

saddlepoint_expansions.taylor is a self-contained implementation of truncated multivariate Taylor polynomials: variables(d, N) returns the \(d\) variables of degree \(N\) as an object array, and any generic function evaluated on them returns a TPoly whose coeff(nu) is \(f^{(\nu)}(0)/\nu!\) and derivative(nu) is \(f^{(\nu)}(0)\). Products use precomputed index tables and np.bincount; a product of two polynomials of degree 12 in three variables takes about 0.1 ms, an exp or log about 1 ms.
